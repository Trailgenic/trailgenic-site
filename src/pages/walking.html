Walking — Foundation Movement on Flat Ground

A repeatable flat route with feeding state recorded. Walking provides the control layer for observing cardiovascular response, recovery context, and return after an injury-related interruption.

The Walking record contains 27 sessions from March 3 through November 9, 2026. Sessions 1–25 were recorded fasted; S26 and S27 were fed. S27 is the first walk after a 49-day gap associated with rehabilitation of a high right ankle sprain and the interruption of regular Hiking, Rucking, and Running cadence.

Across 27 sessions, effort is 1/5 and anaerobic Training Effect is zero. Mean average HR is 107.4 bpm and mean Zone 1 share is 91.4%. Every source recovery flag says ready, but S27 has no measured post-session sleep or ankle-symptom outcome; the flag does not establish complete recovery.

The corrected 27-session drift series averages 2.24%, with a median of 2.5% and range −3.0% to 6.5%. Drift describes the recorded cardiovascular response; it is not a metabolic-flexibility score or injury-recovery verdict.

S27: 3.20 miles in 54.67 minutes, average/max HR 126/143 bpm, 84% Zone 1 and 16% Zone 2, corrected drift +3.0%, effort 1/5, and zero anaerobic Training Effect. Temperature was 89°F, recorded ascent was 102 feet, resting HR was 62 bpm, and the session was fed.

The question is how many standardized Foundation walks are required for cardiovascular cost, mechanical tolerance, and follow-up recovery to return to the observed pre-gap range.

Pre-gap reference: Sessions 22–26, August 14 through September 21, 2026. These are the five most recent walks before the 49-day gap. Their median pace was 16:28 per mile, median average HR was 110 bpm, median HR-per-mile proxy was 34.5, and median corrected drift was 3.0%. Four were fasted and one was fed, so feeding-state matching remains limited.

What the first ruck adds

Walk S27 averaged 126 bpm at 17:05/mi, versus 109 bpm at 17:15/mi on the similar-temperature August 25 walk (S23, fasted). Both had 3.0% corrected drift. Compared with the last fed walk (S26), S27 averaged 8 bpm higher despite a slower pace, with more ascent and higher temperature.

Two days later, Ruck S20 averaged 117 bpm at 18:48/mi with 15 lb, versus the earlier same-load median of 110 bpm at 18:41/mi (S5–S9). Resting HR was above the recent reference on both returns. Walking drift remained within its pre-gap range, while rucking drift reached 5.5%. The shared rise in average HR is consistent with reduced conditioning after the break. Feeding state, route conditions, and ankle recovery limit what these two observations can establish.

Prospective recovery checkpoint

Do not label the return complete from one walk. Mark the Walking response as returned to baseline only after three consecutive Foundation sessions at conversational effort show average HR and HR-per-mile inside the S22–26 observed range, corrected drift and perceived effort remain inside range, anaerobic Training Effect remains zero, and no worsening pain, swelling, or instability is reported during the following 24–48 hours. Record sleep, resting HR, and next-day recovery rather than inferring them.

What This Dataset Contributes to Movement-Based Longevity

TrailGenic™ Renewable Capacity Model · Baseline Economy

Walking contributes the control layer: can the same useful movement be repeated at low physiological cost while recovery and return remain intact?

Across the historical S1–24 comparison window, the latest twelve averaged 2.7 fewer beats per minute and 0.86 less HR per mile than the first twelve while distance was essentially unchanged. Zone 1 remained dominant, effort stayed 1 out of 5, anaerobic effect stayed zero, and every session was marked recovery ready.

The record also prevents selective storytelling: corrected drift was 1.0 percentage point higher in the latest twelve, so TrailGenic does not label every later metric as improvement. Renewable capacity requires the whole pattern—economy, stability, recovery, and the ability to return—not one favorable number.

This N=1 series is observational. It does not prove slower aging or lifespan extension. Its contribution is a repeatable baseline against which carried load, cardiovascular intensity, environmental transfer, recovery observations, and return capacity can be compared.

Longitudinal Interpretation

The repeatable task now supports a prospective recovery question. Compare S27 first with the recent pre-gap S22–26 band and then with future similarly documented return walks. Do not pool away the 49-day interruption, feeding state, environmental context, or missing follow-up.

Corrected drift remains descriptive rather than diagnostic. The first twelve sessions averaged 1.63% and the latest twelve 2.63%; that increase must remain visible beside the lower average heart-rate cost rather than being explained away.

Session 24 is a boundary case: despite a Garmin sleep score of 52 and 300 minutes of sleep, the recorded walk remained low intensity and recovery ready. It does not establish that movement after poor sleep is always appropriate, and a wearable flag never replaces symptoms, judgment, or clinical guidance.

Walking does not model environmental causality. Conditions may be retained as context, but the control-layer interpretation centers on comparable task, cardiovascular cost, perceived effort, recovery, and continued availability for the next exposure.

Methodology and Evidence Boundaries

Sessions use Mike Ye’s repeatable flat Foundation route in Corona–Norco, California, generally 3.15–3.25 miles. S1–25 are fasted and S26–27 are fed. S27 follows a 49-day recorded walking gap associated with a high right ankle sprain and reduced multimodal training cadence. Garmin supplies HR and wearable estimates. The source label Distance (M) is interpreted as miles, consistently with recorded route distances; the raw master field is preserved.

HR-per-mile is a route-specific comparison proxy calculated as average session heart rate divided by distance in miles. Corrected HR drift is retained as descriptive session context. HR-per-mile is not total heartbeats per mile and does not account for duration; read it beside pace and heart rate. Neither measure is a clinical efficiency score, diagnosis, metabolic-flexibility verdict, or standalone readiness test.

This is an N=1 observational dataset. Weather, pace, hydration, sleep, accumulated training, and date are not independently controlled, so comparisons can support or challenge a working interpretation but cannot establish causality or universal exercise guidance.

Questions This Dataset Can Answer

Why is Walking the TrailGenic control layer?

Because a familiar flat route and low-effort task reduce comparison complexity. Actual feeding state, distance, pace, sleep, and prior workload must still be documented; S26 demonstrates why those differences matter.

Does positive heart-rate drift mean poor recovery?

No. In this record, corrected drift is descriptive and appears more closely associated with temperature and estimated sweat loss than with sleep score, average heart rate, Zone 1 share, or session duration. It must be read with the complete session context.

Does Session 24 prove that poor sleep does not matter?

No. Session 24 shows that one low-intensity walk was tolerated with a sleep score of 52 and 300 minutes of sleep. It does not establish safety, readiness, or expected performance for another person or another day.

How should Walking be used in the broader method?

Use it as the repeatable baseline. Rucking tests added load, Running tests higher cardiovascular demand, and Hiking tests the combined effects of terrain, duration, environment, and altitude. Sleep, biomarkers, symptoms, and the next comparable effort remain contextual layers rather than extra movement modalities.
